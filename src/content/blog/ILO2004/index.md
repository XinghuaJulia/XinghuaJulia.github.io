## Problem No. 1: Kayapo

Whenever you encounter a problem like this, the first thing you should do is identify commonalities in both the foreign language and its translation. Here, the recurring pieces are not always whole words, but syllables.

| # | Original | English |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Atoro kêt | You are not dancing |
| 2 | Ba mẽ mẽ anhê | We are decorating you guys |
| 3 | Ba rê | I am swimming |
| 4 | Ga iku | You are devouring me |
| 5 | Ga mẽ to | You guys are dancing |
| 6 | Ije mẽ akuru kêt | I am not devouring you guys |
| 7 | Mẽ aje inhê kêt | You guys are not decorating me |
| 8 | Mẽ irêrê kêt | We are not swimming |

### 1. Identify the repeating syllables

The first one I spotted occurs across four sentences:

- **`kêt`** appears in sentences 1, 6, 7, and 8, so it marks **negation**: “not.”
- **`mẽ`** appears in sentences 2, 5, 6, 7, and 8, so it is a **plural marker**. Sentence 2 contains it twice because both the first-person subject (“we”) and the second-person object (“you guys”) are plural. We will return to sentence structure shortly.

#### Verbs

The verbs are a little trickier. At this stage, I am not yet certain whether a verb consists of one syllable or several, so let us begin by treating the following syllables as the verbal roots:

- **`to`**: dancing (1, 5)
- **`ku`**: devouring (4, 6)
- **`nhê`**: decorating (2, 7)
- **`rê`**: swimming (3, 8)

#### Pronouns

Once the pieces above are identified, the pronouns become much easier to isolate. Combine them with the plural marker **`mẽ`** to form their plural counterparts.

- **`ga`**: “you,” as a standalone word (4, 5)
- **`ba`**: “I,” as a standalone word (2, 3)
- **`a`**: “you,” prefixed to a verb (2, 6)
- **`i`**: “I,” prefixed to a verb (7, 8)

### 2. Work out the sentence structure

Luckily, we do not need to worry about tense: every sentence follows one of two broad patterns.

- **Subject + verb + optional negation**
- **Subject + object + verb + optional negation**

Honestly, once you reach this stage, the rest falls into place. Just watch where the plural marker goes and you will be fine!

## Problem No. 2: Swift News Agency

I would like to disagree with the claim that knowledge of English is unnecessary here. To solve this challenge, you do need to recognise an ambiguity in the word *threaten*.

> **Quick definition**
>
> A **homonym** is a word that shares its spelling and pronunciation with another word but has a different meaning.

For this puzzle, two senses of *threaten* matter:

1. To endanger something.
2. To show signs that something unpleasant may happen or worsen.

Knowing this helps us divide the articles into two groups:

1. **Endanger:** articles 1, 7, 8, and 10  
   Pattern: **cause threatens a desirable outcome**.
2. **Show signs of worsening:** articles 3, 4, 5, and 11  
   Pattern: **cause threatens an undesirable outcome**.

Three effects remain ambiguous:

- the start of the shipping season on small lakes;
- tax reform;
- early elections.

None of these is clearly desirable or undesirable without additional context, so each can be misinterpreted. We therefore need to rewrite those headlines to remove the ambiguity.

**Difficulty:** 🍞🍞

## Problem No. 3: Latin

Yes, problem sets sometimes include languages that are no longer spoken in their historical form. This one was particularly challenging because of one crucial idea: related English and Latin words may preserve the same older sound differently. A consonant from an earlier stage of a language may change or disappear in its descendants.

Here, *cognate* means that two words share a common history, which is why they often retain similar forms.

> **A note on the wording**
>
> I found this question rather awkwardly worded. The first line of information and the first line of Assignment 1 initially led me to think that exactly one of the four listed words lacked the *dh* sound. The intended comparison actually uses the larger list of fourteen words. Having ambiguity in a linguistics puzzle is very meta of them -_-

### Narrowing down the pattern

- **`rb` corresponds to `rd`** in pairs such as *beard* and *word*.
- When **`d`** in the English word falls between two vowels, as in *divides*, *widow*, and *moderate*, the **`d`** is preserved in Latin.
- *Sediment* is the one exception I kept in view: instead of preserving the **`d`**, it seems almost to disappear. I wonder why...

Assuming the other cognates preserve the **`d`**, three words still do not fit neatly into the patterns above: *mandere*, *mordax*, and *glabra*.

In the pair involving *red paint*, **`br`** shows that the letter **`d`** is preserved. That lets us eliminate *glabra*.

We are now left with *mandere* and *mordax*. Since words containing **`rb`** correspond to English **`rd`**, the *dh* sound in *mordax* must never have existed. Therefore, the odd one out is **mordax**. Following the same logic, a Latin word containing **`rd`** should not contain the *dh* sound.

### The words beginning with *f*

F this. Let us get that out of the way first.

Recall *sediment*, the exception we kept in view: its corresponding form is the only one with an **`fr`** prefix. The remaining words whose English cognates lack **`d`** begin with **`f`** followed by a vowel. From this, we can infer that words beginning with **`f`** contain the *dh* sound. It also means that a Latin word beginning with **`d`** should not preserve that sound; otherwise, it would have begun with **`f`** instead.

Phew. That was a tough one, easily the hardest question on the paper for me.

**Difficulty:** 🍞🍞🍞🍞

## Problem No. 4: Lakhota

The key to this question is **degree**: a single consonant can signal the severity or intensity of a property and act as a modifier within the word.

| # | Lakhota | English |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | kĩza | a single high-pitched tone sounds |
| 2 | žata | it *(e.g. a road)* forks into two parts |
| 3 | šuža | it is badly bruised |
| 4 | γi | it is brown |
| 5 | mⁱniža | it is curled but can be smoothed again |
| 6 | gᵉleža | it is ruled: \|\|\| |
| 7 | nuγa | it is hard and immovable *(e.g. a gnarl on a tree)* |
| 8 | mⁱniγa | it is shrunk permanently |
| 9 | zi | it is yellow |
| 10 | šli | thick liquid is being squeezed out |
| 11 | kĩža | a blending high-pitched tone sounds *(e.g. a trill)* |
| 12 | gᵉleγa | it is striped: █ █ █ |

### Compare the consonants

| Comparison | Evidence | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| **`z` vs. `ž`** | *kĩza* is one high-pitched tone, while *kĩža* is a blend of high-pitched tones. | **`z < ž`** |
| **`ž` vs. `γ`** | *gᵉleža* has light ruled lines, while *gᵉleγa* has bold, dark stripes. | **`ž < γ`** |
| **`ž` vs. `γ`** | *mⁱniža* describes a temporary change, while *mⁱniγa* describes a permanent one. | **`ž < γ`** |

Chaining those comparisons together gives us the relationship:

> **`z < ž < γ`**

With that, the rest of the puzzle should be smooth sailing. We are back to basic pattern spotting: the main part of each word determines its meaning, while the contrasting consonant determines its degree.

**Difficulty:** 🍞🍞🍞

## Problem No. 5: Chuvash

The intended solution relies on vowel harmony, but with some astute pattern spotting and the clues provided, it is possible to solve the problem without knowing the concept beforehand. You can then revisit it with that knowledge and gain a more complete view of the puzzle.

> **Quick definition**
>
> **Vowel harmony** occurs when a vowel in one part of a word affects the vowels in other parts of that same word. The pattern may depend on features such as roundedness, height, and frontness or backness.

Let us get the obvious distinction out of the way: transitive and intransitive verbs. The left column contains **intransitive verbs**, in which the subject undergoes the change and no direct object is required. The right column contains **transitive verbs**, in which the subject causes that change in an object.

The full solution should be fairly straightforward from there. Just note that multiple answers are sometimes acceptable, so I would say the setter was rather kind with this one.

## Review

I have now looked at five years of these contests. It is not a huge sample, but it is enough to notice a general structure. The papers usually include some combination of:

- **Number problems**, especially in more recent years: deciphering how a language handles counting, numbers, or dates.
- **Sentence or word mapping:** translating from the examples provided. You may also be asked to construct new sentences in the language.

The main challenge lies in spotting patterns quickly and noticing minuscule details: accents, affixes, and sentence structure. Occasionally, some linguistics knowledge is useful, as shown above, where the properties of vowels and consonants govern how words are formed. Still, most of these challenges are very doable without an in-depth knowledge of the language.

Of course, as a colleague aptly pointed out:

> *What if I know the language? Isn't that an unfair advantage?*

Well, yes and no. I initially brushed off his concern, only to be hit with IOL 2003 Problem 5, which was entirely in French. Lovely.

Sure, it may be an advantage if you happen to speak that particular language or one from the same language family. However, most papers feature a wide variety of languages whose speaker populations range from mere thousands to millions. Regardless of familiarity, one can still appreciate the method of dissecting each problem, which brings me to...

### The whole idea of solving puzzles

To me, once you find an attack vector, the key or turning point, the puzzle collapses from a complex problem with frayed ends into an organised problem. If you take anything away from this, it is to not be afraid to keep cracking your head at it.

If you are stuck, take a walk. Go outside. See the sun. Let the idea sink in for a bit. Maybe, just maybe, when you return to the problem, you will see a fresh angle you had not considered before :>
